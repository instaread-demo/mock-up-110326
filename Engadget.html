Dell XPS Googlebook review: Beautiful hardware for a useless platform

Googlebook OS still needs a ton of work.

By Devindra Hardawar Nov. 2, 2026 7:30 am EST
Devindra Hardawar for Engadget

I can think of a few reasons to buy a Googlebook, but they're all pretty dystopian. Maybe every other computing platform has succumbed to a super AI-powered virus, leaving a Googlebook as your only option. Or perhaps Google has taken over the world and has started hunting down people who use Windows, Linux or macOS. In a world where a Googlebook is your only option, then the $1,000 Dell XPS Googlebook is probably the one to get. But in the real world, where you can get the exact same XPS hardware running on more flexible platforms with better developer support, the XPS Googlebook seems practically pointless.

But let's back up a bit and talk about Googlebook OS, Google's new laptop platform that's a combination of both Android and ChromeOS. It supports Android apps natively, it can synchronize and mirror apps from your Android phone, and it has Google's Gemini AI integrated throughout its interface. Technically, Googlebook OS should be an upgrade from ChromeOS, a platform built entirely around Google's web browser. But, after testing this laptop for the last week, I'd go so far as to say the entire Googlebook concept is a failure.

Who really wants a laptop running unoptimized Android apps? Why buy into an untested Google platform just to synchronize your Android phone, especially when you can mirror and sync Android device texts on Windows and Linux? And why would you pay the same exact price as a Windows PC for a computer that does so much less? While Apple, Microsoft and PC makers are sweating to push the computing industry forward this year, most notably with the launch of NVIDIA's RTX Spark and Apple's redesigned touchscreen MacBook Pro, Google has given us a new platform that is in many ways less useful than its aging Chromebooks.

What's special about the Dell XPS Googlebook?

Devindra Hardawar for Engadget

For its first (and I'm hoping only) Googlebook, Dell took its recent XPS 13 design and refashioned it for Google's new OS. It still has a slim 2.2-pound metal case, making it one of the lightest systems in this size range, and it sports a 13.4-inch InfinityEdge screen. It's powered by a Qualcomm Snapdragon X Elite chip with 16GB of RAM — the same hardware on the $1,000 Windows 11 XPS 13. There are only two USB-C ports on either side of the XPS 13 Googlebook, and, unfortunately, there's no headphone jack.

A multi-colored "Glowbar" occasionally lights up on the rear of the XPS 13 — something that Google highlights as one of the defining features of Googlebook OS — but it's ultimately superfluous. It can show your charging status and battery level, but it's not exactly solving any major problems for laptop users.

It's not unusual to see PC makers reusing their designs — many companies are also recycling their existing work for NVIDIA's RTX Spark chip. But at least that platform has a few halo products designed specifically for it, like the Surface Laptop Ultra. There's nothing highlighting what's truly special about Googlebook OS — arguably because there's not exactly much to show off.

In-use: A pretty frame around a pointless platform

Devindra Hardawar for Engadget

I could tell you that the XPS Googlebook has a very nice screen, a comfortable keyboard and a very accurate touchpad. I could tell you that its slim design and light weight makes it easy to tote around without weighing down your bag. But really, you could have learned all of that from my review of the standard XPS 13 with Windows 11. Everything good about this system comes from Dell's excellent design work.

Once I started using Googlebook OS, though, it became instantly clear that Googlebook OS is simply a hot mess. It's fine for web browsing with Chrome and accessing Google apps like Gmail and Calendar, but support for Android apps via the Google Play store is mostly disappointing. Slack only works as a mobile Android app, allowing you to see a single channel or direct message, but there's no wider desktop view like you'd see on Windows or macOS. You also can't easily react to messages and images in Slack — instead, you have to perform a long click, as you would on a normal Android phone. Evernote, meanwhile, doesn't appear at all on the Googlebook Play Store. (And yes, I still use Evernote a ton. There are dozens of us!)

"Desktop optimized" Android apps function more like you'd expect on other laptop platforms. Notability, CapCut Pad and Luminar all offer more fully-featured apps for taking AI notes and editing media, but they're only useful if those apps are already part of your workflow. I can't imagine too many people will change their editing process just to find something compatible with Googlebook OS.

Using the XPS Googlebook for my typical workflow often felt like pulling teeth. While Chrome worked fine for web browsing, resizing a photo required an odyssey through several different apps until I stumbled into Pixlr, a desktop-optimized editing app that only lets you resize an image when exporting it. Adobe Lightroom only seemed to let me resize a picture by cropping it (and it also kept bugging me to subscribe to Lightroom Premium to unlock more editing features). I eventually discovered that the standalone Viewer app has an easy way to resize images, something that isn't present in the default Photos app.

With no support for Audacity, I had to use Lexis Audio Editor to make some tweaks to a podcast file, but its basic interface seemed unsuited to more complicated edits. I might as well have just used an online editor like Audiomass (which, by the way, works just fine on Chromebooks). I also had to rely on Evernote's website to write this review, which required logging in every single time I turned on the XPS Googlebook, because the Evernote Android app was unavailable. I also eventually gave up on the Slack Android app and started using its web interface, which at least let me see all of my channels and direct messages.

I'm used to computers being gateways to sophisticated software and healthy developer support. Using the XPS Googlebook felt like being trapped in a computing stripmall: It's hobbled together by a variety of small apps of varying quality. Thank goodness for web apps, at least.

It's worth noting that Chromebooks also supported Android apps, so I don't believe there was actually a huge demand for a new Android-based computing platform.

I also wasn't impressed by any of the other Googlebook features: Syncing your Android phone to a Googlebook sounds nice in theory, but in reality it leads to redundant notifications if you have the same apps installed on both systems. You can also mirror an Android app from your phone — but again, why would you do that when you can run the app directly on the Googlebook? Given that you can already mirror Android phones and synchronize texts on Windows computers, I'm not sure why anyone would jump into a whole new platform just for these features.

Devindra Hardawar for Engadget

Google being Google, of course, the company also crammed in as many Gemini AI features as it could into this platform. You can ask questions or edit media on the Gemini app, or wiggle the mouse pointer to ask the AI about things you encounter on the web or inside of apps. Gemini did a decent job of answering questions about products that we've featured on Engadget, but unless you absolutely want to lean on Google's AI, there really is no reason to buy a whole laptop just to have Gemini baked into everything.

Google's Rambler AI dictation feature, which lets you dictate text by speaking naturally, is also built into Googlebook OS, and from my testing it seemed to work pretty well. But it doesn't show you the text as you're speaking — it just shows a moving purple waveform, and that sometimes makes it hard to gather your thoughts or see what you were previously thinking. This is a big downside compared to just typing or using old-school dictation that used to be available on Android, but at least Rambler did a good job of cutting out natural "ums" and "uhhs," and it also formatted the text pretty well.

Battery life is another sign that the Googlebook OS platform is very unrefined. In the PCMark Android app benchmark, it lasted for 15 hours and 15 minutes, which is what we'd expect for a Snapdragon laptop. But during normal usage, I found that some apps ate up a lot of battery power without much reason. CapCut Pad managed to drain my battery by 50% in just one hour, even though it was simply sitting in the background, not doing anything. I simply couldn't trust the XPS Googlebook to last for a full day of work.

Should you buy the XPS Googlebook?

Devindra Hardawar for Engadget

While the XPS Googlebook looks like a premium laptop, the fact that it's running an undercooked OS makes it something I can't recommend. Just get the $999 XPS 13 with Windows 11 instead — at least that gets you a mature OS that runs tons of apps and games. None of this is Dell's fault, as its XPS hardware is once again among the best in the PC world. But that doesn't matter if it's paired with yet another flimsy Google platform.

Sure, Googlebook OS will get more compatible Android apps down the line, and it may eventually turn into something more useful with upcoming AI features. But you shouldn't rush out to pay $999 with the hopes that it will get better. Why pay to be a beta tester? Google also hasn't been the best steward of its troubled platforms in the past, so I wouldn't be too surprised if Googlebook OS simply fades away due to lack of interest. This is the same company that couldn't optimize Android for tablets over the last decade.

Wrap-up

Devindra Hardawar for Engadget

The Googlebook launch may go down as one of the more baffling moments in computing history. Instead of delivering a true successor to Chromebooks – something that was similarly inexpensive, efficient and school-ready, but also ready for useful AI features – Google delivered a half-baked platform that's in many ways a step back. Not even Dell's excellent design can save the XPS Googlebook from the mess of Googlebook OS.

Don't throw away your old router — Do this instead

Your old router has a lot of potential on your network, so give it a new job.

By Calvin Wankhede Sept. 29, 2026 8:00 pm EST
Vladimir Soldatov/Getty Images

Unlike many devices in your home, you probably don't think much about upgrading your router. Routers can work for a long time, and it's easy to overlook when one isn't getting firmware updates anymore or doesn't support the latest Wi-Fi standards. This can make upgrading to a new Wi-Fi router more difficult, since throwing away a perfectly functioning piece of equipment feels wasteful.

Thankfully, this doesn't need to be an issue. Old routers can easily be repurposed to have use even as a secondary or tertiary device. While newer models offer better security, range, and performance and are thus the best for your primary device, old routers can take advantage of the fact that most networking fundamentals haven't changed in decades. You have many ways to repurpose an old router instead of sending it off to the landfill or for recycling.

Turn your old router into a Wi-Fi repeater or mesh node

If your new Wi-Fi router struggles to reach corners of your home, consider using your old router as a dedicated access point. With this method, you don't need to buy a wireless extender or another single-purpose piece of tech.

There are two approaches you can take: wired or wireless. A wired connection brings stable connectivity and fast speeds, but you need an Ethernet line running back to your primary router. This might not be possible several rooms away unless you're comfortable pulling cable through your walls. 

I recommend against broadcasting two Wi-Fi signals in close proximity, since this can lead to wireless interference and worse performance. The good news is that you can disable specific frequencies on both routers to avoid congestion. A good option is configuring the old router to broadcast on the 2.4GHz band, while disabling that frequency on the newer router. This is because 2.4GHz signals penetrate walls better, while most of the Wi-Fi improvements over the past decade have applied to higher frequencies like 5GHz and 6GHz.

Many routers support wireless repeater or bridge functionality, so check the admin panel or manual for those terms. On my older TP-Link router, this feature is hidden within several sub-menus under the WDS bridging label. The downside with this approach is that your router has to use part of its limited wireless capacity to communicate with your router. This translates to slower real-world speeds when you're connected to the secondary router.

A small number of routers can also double up as a mesh Wi-Fi node. Asus calls this feature AiMesh on its routers, and it allows you to mix different models to create one unified network. If you haven't chosen a new router yet and your old one offers this, buy one in the same family to take advantage of it.

Use the LAN ports for an easy network switch

sweet_tomato/Shutterstock

If your new router doesn't have enough LAN ports to connect high-bandwidth devices like smart TVs, computers and gaming consoles, you can use your old router as a switch. A basic network switch typically costs between $10 and $20, and is yet another single-use device you don't need to buy if you have an old router lying around.

Setting up a router in switch mode is relatively easy. Simply run an Ethernet cable from your new router to the old one, using LAN ports on each, and connect your devices to the remaining ports on the old router. Make sure you don't plug anything into the old router's WAN port.

Many routers offer Access Point (AP) or Switch modes in their administrative settings, which you'll have to enable by accessing the router's admin panel. If you don't see one of these modes, you'll have to disable the router's DHCP server, along with any other routing options like firewall and NAT, manually.

The same warning about wireless interference applies here as well. You should disable all wireless functionality on the old router if you only care about the onboard Ethernet ports.

Create file and print servers for wireless convenience

STOCKEROcr/Shutterstock

Many routers have a USB port that's easy to overlook. But you can use this in many ways, including to connect a storage device or printer. The latter allows you to initiate print jobs from any device connected to your router, even wirelessly. If you have an old printer that lacks Wi-Fi support, this can be an easy workaround.

Connecting a storage device like an external hard drive or thumb drive can turn your router into a rudimentary storage server. In other words, you can read and write to the storage device from any device on the network. Dedicated network-attached storage (NAS) machines and the drives for them cost a fair bit, so this is an inexpensive way to get started. Just keep in mind that routers don't include any reliability or backup features, which is where actual NAS devices shine. And the USB port on your router is likely not the fastest, so performance will be limited. 

After connecting the device, poke around in your router's admin panel to check its USB device sharing settings for the best experience.

Turn it into a dedicated VPN router

Many modern routers support VPN functionality, but enabling this feature means routing all traffic through the tunnel. You can install your VPN app on individual devices instead, but that might lead to battery drain over long periods. This is where repurposing an old router into a VPN client makes a lot of sense. Using a dedicated router for the VPN that operates independently from your primary network means you can connect to it when you want, without running all traffic through it or forcing your mobile devices to handle the workload.

The exact instructions vary by VPN provider and router manufacturer, but you'll generally start by connecting your old router to the new one and enabling access point mode. Then, look for VPN settings in the router's administration panel, turn on the feature, and provide your VPN credentials.

Install custom firmware for maximum flexibility

SOLDATOOFF/Shutterstock

While we don't always think about them in this way, routers are essentially low-power computers, typically pairing an efficient CPU with small amounts of RAM and memory. They accordingly ship with lightweight operating systems designed for their intended purpose: routing network traffic and everything else associated with that task. This creates problems when manufacturers stop providing the latest firmware for your router, or when its limited nature prevents you from doing what you want.

Luckily for us, entire enterprising open-source communities have built custom firmware designed specifically for routers. Projects like OpenWRT replace a router's manufacturer-provided software with a more flexible OS that gives you greater control over the device, including the ability to install third-party apps like torrent clients. 

These are a great option to keep your old router going for longer. Many of the suggestions above are also easier to set up on custom router firmware like DD-WRT or FreshTomato. I use OpenWRT, as I find it less restrictive than many routers' default operating systems.

The downside to custom firmware is that not all routers are supported. Given the sheer number of models, you'll have to read through each project's documentation to narrow down compatibility for your specific device.
